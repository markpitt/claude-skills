## Macbeth (Shakespeare)

**Exam board availability:** AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas

### Key Themes
| Theme | Key Focus |
|-------|----------|
| **Ambition** | Macbeth's unchecked ambition drives the tragedy; ambition is presented as corrupting |
| **Guilt and conscience** | Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking; Macbeth's visions (the dagger, Banquo's ghost); inability to say "Amen" |
| **The supernatural** | The Witches as agents of fate or mirrors of desire; "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" |
| **Appearance vs. reality** | Deception runs throughout: "Look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't" |
| **Masculinity and gender** | Lady Macbeth rejects femininity to gain power; Macbeth's masculinity is weaponised against him |
| **Power and kingship** | Divine Right of Kings; tyranny vs. legitimate rule; the chain of being disrupted |

### Key Characters
- **Macbeth:** Track his psychological decline from "valiant" soldier to tyrannical ruler. His bravery at the start (Sergeant's report) vs. desperation at the end ("Give me my armour"). He is never fully at ease — guilt prevents peace.
- **Lady Macbeth:** Begins more ruthless than Macbeth ("unsex me here"); loses power as Macbeth grows more independent; ends in psychological collapse (sleepwalking). A subversion of Jacobean gender expectations.
- **The Witches:** Deliberately ambiguous — do they cause events or reflect desires? Their equivocation ("none of woman born") reveals how prophecy can deceive.
- **Banquo:** Foil to Macbeth — receives the same prophecy but chooses differently. His ghost represents Macbeth's guilt.

### Top Exam Technique Points
- Focus on Shakespeare's use of **tragedy as a form** — Macbeth's downfall is the inevitable consequence of disorder (regicide, disrupting the natural order, defying the Divine Right of Kings)
- **Jacobean context**: James I believed in witchcraft and the Divine Right of Kings — audiences would have seen Macbeth's actions as deeply transgressive
- Don't just discuss "ambition" — the most sophisticated responses explore the interplay between fate and free will: to what extent is Macbeth responsible for his own destruction?

### Key Quotations (short, embeddable)
- "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" (Witches — ambiguity established immediately)
- "valiant Macbeth" (Sergeant — establishing heroic status before corruption)
- "Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires" (Macbeth — concealment/desire)
- "unsex me here" (Lady Macbeth — rejection of femininity/gender subversion)
- "a dagger of the mind, a false creation" (Macbeth — guilt manifesting as hallucination)
- "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" (Macbeth — guilt)
- "Out, damned spot!" (Lady Macbeth — psychological collapse)
- "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" (Macbeth — nihilism, loss of meaning)

## An Inspector Calls (J.B. Priestley)

**Exam board availability:** AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas

### Key Themes
| Theme | Key Focus |
|-------|----------|
| **Social responsibility** | Priestley's central message: we are all responsible for one another |
| **Class and capitalism** | The Birlings represent upper-class insulation from consequences; Eva Smith = working class victim |
| **Generational divide** | Sheila and Eric can change; Mr and Mrs Birling cannot — who holds hope for the future? |
| **Gender** | Sheila and Mrs Birling contrasted; Eva's vulnerability is partly due to her gender |
| **Guilt and conscience** | Each character is implicated; the Inspector forces accountability |
| **Time** | Written in 1945, set in 1912 — Priestley's dramatic irony of dramatic irony (WW1, the Titanic) |

### Key Characters
- **Inspector Goole:** Catalyst, not detective — he extracts confessions rather than gathering evidence. His name ("Goole" sounds like "ghoul") hints he may be supernatural: a collective conscience. May represent God, death, or moral reckoning.
- **Mr Birling:** Embodiment of capitalistic arrogance. His early long speeches establish his pomposity; dramatic irony undercuts him (he predicts no war, calls the Titanic "unsinkable"). Refuses to change at the end — defends his position.
- **Mrs Birling:** Represents class prejudice and emotional coldness. Her responsibility for Eva's death is perhaps the most callous — she denies her own grandchild's father responsibility.
- **Sheila:** Represents hope and change. Transforms from complicit to moral by the end; her journey IS Priestley's argument that the younger generation can build a better world.
- **Eric:** Hidden vice (drinking, assault) but genuine remorse. Like Sheila, he ultimately accepts responsibility.

### Context: Why Priestley Set the Play in 1912 but Wrote It in 1945
- Writing after two world wars, Priestley wanted to prevent the selfishness of the Edwardian upper class from poisoning the post-war world
- The welfare state was being built (NHS founded 1948) — Priestley was advocating for social democracy
- The play is a morality piece: the Birlings' world of privilege is being judged

### Staging and Structure
- **Setting:** One room, one evening — claustrophobic; no escape from responsibility
- **Dramatic irony:** The audience knows WW1 and WW2 happened when Birling boasts about peace
- **Cyclical structure:** The phone rings at the end — the cycle of accountability continues. Justice cannot be escaped.
- **Lighting:** Priestley's stage direction specifies "pink and intimate" at first, changing to "brighter and harder" when the Inspector arrives — the comfortable illusions are stripped away

### Key Quotations
- "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself" (Birling — capitalist worldview, the position Priestley attacks)
- "cheap labour" (Birling on Eva Smith — dehumanisation through economic language)
- "If men will not learn that lesson, then they will be taught it in fire and blood and anguish" (Inspector — prophesies WW1 and WW2)
- "We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other." (Inspector — Priestley's thesis)
- "Girls of that class..." (Mrs Birling — class contempt)
- "You're beginning to pretend now that nothing's really happened" (Sheila — refusal to retreat)

## The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (Stevenson)

**Exam board availability:** AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas

### Key Themes
| Theme | Key Focus |
|-------|----------|
| **Duality of human nature** | Every person contains good and evil — Hyde is the hidden self unleashed |
| **Victorian repression** | Victorian upper-class society demanded respectability; Hyde is what is suppressed |
| **Science vs. religion** | Jekyll's experiment transgresses natural and divine law; Darwinism disturbed Victorian certainty |
| **Secrecy and shame** | The men of the novel keep secrets; Utterson is told to "ask no questions" — a culture of concealment |
| **Degeneration** | Victorian fear that civilised humanity could revert to a more primitive state |
| **Appearance vs. reality** | Hyde is physically repellent; Jekyll is well-regarded — but Hyde is Jekyll |

### Narrative Structure
- Told through **multiple narrators** (Utterson, Lanyon's letter, Jekyll's confession) — fragmented truth, unreliable perspectives, secrets revealed gradually
- **Gothic genre conventions**: fog, darkness, locked doors, mystery — used to mirror psychological concealment
- The reader doesn't learn the full truth until Jekyll's final statement — Stevenson structures revelation to maximise shock

### Context: Victorian Society
- **1886 publication:** Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) had disrupted religious certainty — is humanity just a higher animal?
- **Degeneration theory:** Victorian scientists feared civilised society could "regress" — Hyde physically embodies this fear
- **The importance of reputation:** Victorian professional men could be ruined by scandal — this explains why Utterson covers things up, why Jekyll cannot admit Hyde

### Key Quotations
- "That child of Hell had nothing human; nothing lived in him but fear and hatred" (Utterson on Hyde)
- "trampled calmly over the child's body" (Hyde's first act — callousness, inhumanity)
- "If I am the chief of sinners, I am the chief of sufferers also" (Jekyll — victim and perpetrator simultaneously)
- "I learned to recognise the thorough and primitive duality of man" (Jekyll — the text's central idea)
- "man is not truly one, but truly two" (Jekyll — duality is universal, not peculiar to Jekyll)
- "like a man disinterred" (Jekyll after Hyde's period of dominance — language of death/burial, the self lost)

## Power and Conflict Poetry Anthology (AQA)

### Overview of the 15 Poems

| Poem | Poet | Key Focus |
|------|------|----------|
| *Ozymandias* | Percy Bysshe Shelley | Power of nature over human arrogance; impermanence of political power |
| *London* | William Blake | Oppression, institutional power, urban misery, loss of freedom |
| *The Prelude* (extract) | William Wordsworth | Nature's sublime power over human beings; humility |
| *My Last Duchess* | Robert Browning | Male control, possessive jealousy, power over women, dramatic monologue |
| *The Charge of the Light Brigade* | Alfred Lord Tennyson | Heroism and futility of war; obedience vs. sacrifice |
| *Exposure* | Wilfred Owen | The brutal reality of WW1; nature as the true enemy, not the Germans |
| *Storm on the Island* | Seamus Heaney | Nature's overwhelming and arbitrary power; fear |
| *Bayonet Charge* | Ted Hughes | The chaos and terror of war; instinctive survival stripping away idealism |
| *Remains* | Simon Armitage | PTSD; the lasting psychological damage of conflict; civilian casualty guilt |
| *Poppies* | Jane Weir | A mother's grief; personal cost of war; memory and loss |
| *War Photographer* | Carol Ann Duffy | Distance and desensitisation; moral responsibility of the media |
| *Tissue* | Imtiaz Dharker | Power of ordinary objects vs. human constructs; fragility of human power |
| *The Emigree* | Carol Rumens | Identity, exile, memory, and the power of imagination |
| *Kamikaze* | Beatrice Garland | Conflict between duty and personal survival; the power of societal expectation |
| *Checking Out Me History* | John Agard | Reclaiming cultural identity; colonial power over education and history |

### Comparison Themes for Power and Conflict

**Power of nature vs. human arrogance:** Ozymandias + Storm on the Island + The Prelude

**Individual vs. institution/state:** London + Checking Out Me History + Kamikaze

**Psychological effects of war:** Remains + War Photographer + Bayonet Charge + Exposure

**Female perspectives on conflict:** Poppies + Kamikaze + War Photographer

**Impermanence of power:** Ozymandias + Tissue + My Last Duchess

**Identity under oppression:** Checking Out Me History + The Emigree + London

### Key Poetic Techniques for Anthology Poems
- **Dramatic monologue** (*My Last Duchess*, *Ozymandias* frame): Consider who is speaking and what they reveal unintentionally — irony through unreliable narrator
- **Free verse** (*Remains*, *War Photographer*): Lack of formal structure can mirror chaos/disorder
- **Sonnet/structured forms** (*Ozymandias*, *Exposure*): Consider whether tight structure is being used ironically (order vs. chaos content)
- **Enjambment**: Often mimics the unstoppable momentum of violence or memory
- **Volta** (turning point): In *Exposure*, the refrain "But nothing happens" — the expected climax never comes

## A Christmas Carol (Dickens)

**Exam board availability:** AQA, Edexcel, Eduqas, OCR

### Key Themes
- **Redemption and transformation:** Scrooge's journey from miser to philanthropist — Dickens argues people CAN change
- **Social responsibility and poverty:** Dickens directly attacks Victorian indifference to the poor; Ignorance and Want beneath the Ghost of Christmas Present's robe
- **Christmas as moral symbol:** The Cratchits' warmth despite poverty vs. Scrooge's cold wealth
- **Time and memory:** The structure uses past/present/future to show how choices compound

### Key Quotations
- "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner" (Dickens' narrator on Scrooge — accumulation of verbs shows excess)
- "Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses?" (Scrooge — echoes Malthusian attitude Dickens attacks)
- "I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel" (Scrooge transformed — childlike joy restored)
- "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both" (Ghost of Christmas Present — social warning)
- "Mankind was my business" (Marley's Ghost — the text's moral thesis)

## Lord of the Flies (Golding)

**Exam board availability:** AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas

### Key Themes
- **Civilisation vs. savagery:** The boys' descent mirrors Golding's belief that evil is inherent in humanity
- **Loss of innocence:** The boys begin as schoolchildren; by the end murder has occurred
- **Power and leadership:** Ralph (democracy/reason) vs. Jack (tribalism/fear/violence)
- **The beast:** The beast is not external — the boys ARE the beast ("Simon's discovery")

### Key Quotations
- "Ralph wept for the end of innocence, the darkness of man's heart" (conclusion — Golding's thesis made explicit)
- "Kill the pig, cut her throat, bash her in" (chant — language stripped to primitive violence)
- "Maybe there is a beast... maybe it's only us" (Simon — truth the other boys cannot face)
- "I'm frightened. Of us." (Ralph — recognition of the real danger)

## Further Study Support

For any text not listed above, ask the student to share their exam question or topic and work through it using the same framework:

1. Identify the key themes or character being asked about
2. Choose 2–3 short, precise quotations
3. Analyse the writer's methods (language, structure, form)
4. Connect to relevant context without "context dumping"
5. Shape the response around the specific question wording

## The PEEL Structure (for all explanation and essay questions)

PEEL is the standard paragraph structure for all analytical writing in GCSE History:

**P — Point**
State your argument clearly in one sentence. Start with the key concept (cause, consequence, etc.) and link it directly to the question.
> *"One reason why Hitler was able to consolidate power was the Reichstag Fire of 1933, which allowed him to dramatically extend his authority."*

**E — Evidence**
Provide specific, precise historical facts: dates, names, statistics, events, terms.
> *"On 27 February 1933, the Reichstag building was set on fire. Marinus van der Lubbe, a Dutch communist, was arrested. Hitler immediately blamed the Communist Party."*

**E — Explanation**
Explain *how* the evidence proves your point. Connect the specific facts to the argument. Use causal language: "This meant that...", "As a result...", "Consequently..."
> *"This meant that Hitler could issue the Reichstag Fire Decree the very next day, suspending civil liberties and allowing the arrest of over 4,000 Communist Party members — removing his main political opposition before the crucial March 1933 elections."*

**L — Link**
Connect back to the question, and (in essays) link forward to your next paragraph or overall argument.
> *"This was significant because it allowed Hitler to manipulate the political system to destroy opposition, which was far more important than [next factor]."*

## Useful Causal and Analytical Language

Use these phrases to show analytical thinking rather than narrative:

**Causation:** "This led to...", "As a direct result of...", "This caused...", "This contributed to...", "A key reason was..."

**Consequence:** "The impact of this was...", "This resulted in...", "The consequence was...", "This meant that...", "This changed..."

**Significance:** "This was significant because...", "This was particularly important as...", "This was a turning point because..."

**Comparison:** "Similarly...", "In contrast...", "Unlike...", "Whereas...", "Both X and Y...", "However..."

**Judgement:** "The most important factor was...", "Overall, the evidence suggests...", "While X was significant, Y was more significant because...", "The primary cause was..."

## Source Analysis: The CUPS Framework (AO3)

Use CUPS for "How useful is Source X for an enquiry into Y?" questions.

**C — Content**
What does the source explicitly show, describe, or tell you? Quote or paraphrase directly.
> *"The source shows a photograph of unemployed men queuing outside a soup kitchen in 1931, suggesting widespread poverty during the Depression."*

**U — Usefulness**
How does the content help answer the *specific* enquiry in the question? (Always link back to the exact question asked — mark schemes reward this.)
> *"This is useful for an enquiry into the effects of the Depression in America because it provides visual evidence of mass unemployment and poverty that the Hoover government failed to address."*

**P — Provenance**
Analyse the origin: Who made it? When? Why? For what audience? How does this affect its usefulness?
> *"The photograph was taken by Dorothea Lange, a documentary photographer employed by the Farm Security Administration in 1936. As a government-commissioned image intended to build public support for New Deal policies, it may deliberately portray suffering to justify government intervention — which limits its usefulness as objective evidence but makes it highly useful for understanding Franklin Roosevelt's political messaging."*

**S — Support (Contextual Knowledge)**
Use your own historical knowledge to confirm, challenge, or add context to what the source shows.
> *"This source is supported by historical statistics showing that unemployment reached 25% in 1933, and historical accounts of 'Hoovervilles' confirm that makeshift camps appeared across US cities."*

### Critical Principle: Usefulness ≠ Reliability

This is the most common mistake in AO3 questions. A source can be:
- **Biased but highly useful** — e.g. Nazi propaganda is unreliable as fact but *very* useful for studying Nazi methods, ideology, and techniques
- **Reliable but limited** — e.g. a private diary may be honest but covers only one perspective
- **Useful for the enquiry despite limitations** — always evaluate *usefulness for this specific enquiry*, not general reliability

## Interpretation Analysis: The DACRE Framework (AO4)

Use DACRE for "How convincing is Interpretation A?" or "How far do you agree with Interpretation B?" questions.

**D — Detail**
Summarise the interpretation's main argument in one or two sentences. Quote precisely.
> *"Interpretation A argues that Hitler's rise to power was inevitable due to the inherent weaknesses of the Weimar Republic, claiming that 'the Republic was doomed from its birth in defeat and humiliation.'"*

**A — Agreement**
Use your own knowledge to support the interpretation. Provide at least one specific, precise piece of evidence.
> *"This is convincing because... The Dolchstosslegende (stab-in-the-back myth) immediately undermined the Republic's legitimacy, and the punishing terms of the Treaty of Versailles created economic instability that later exploded in the hyperinflation crisis of 1923."*

**C — Challenge**
Use your own knowledge to challenge or qualify the interpretation. Again, be specific.
> *"However, the interpretation is less convincing because... By 1929 the Republic had stabilised under Stresemann, with the Dawes Plan reducing reparations and unemployment falling. The Depression was an unpredictable external factor that re-destabilised the Republic — suggesting the collapse was not inevitable but circumstantial."*

**R — Reach**
Make an overall judgement. Is the interpretation convincing? Partly? What is its main strength or weakness?
> *"Overall, the interpretation is partly convincing. While the Republic's early structural weaknesses did make Hitler's rise *possible*, the Great Depression was the decisive trigger — without it, the Nazis might have remained a fringe party."*

**E — Explain the View**
Why did the historian form this interpretation? Consider: their background, methodology, the evidence they emphasised, when they wrote, their intended audience. Avoid generic phrases like "benefit of hindsight."
> *"The historian may emphasise structural causes because they are writing from a constitutional history perspective, focusing on institutions rather than contingent events. Writing in 1962, they may have been influenced by Cold War debates about whether democracy could survive under pressure."*

### Common AO4 Mistakes to Avoid
- "With the benefit of hindsight..." — vague and unrewarded; be specific about what extra knowledge matters
- "It was peer-reviewed so it is reliable" — irrelevant to interpretation questions
- "The historian was biased" — without specifying *what* bias and *how* it shapes the argument
- "They weren't there" — all historians work from evidence; this is not a valid critique

## Extended Essays: "How Far Do You Agree?" (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

This questions tests all four AOs simultaneously and is worth the most marks. Structure it as follows:

### Introduction (3–5 sentences)
- Define the key terms in the question
- State your overall judgement clearly (agree/disagree/partially agree)
- Identify the factors you will discuss and your criterion for ranking them
> *"I largely agree that [X was the main cause], because... However, [Y] and [Z] also contributed significantly. The most important factor was X because... while Y was important, it was more of an enabling condition than a direct cause."*

### Paragraph 1 — The Factor in the Question
Always write a PEEL paragraph addressing the specific statement in the question. Even if you ultimately disagree, engage with the strongest version of the argument.

### Paragraphs 2 and 3 — Other Factors
Write one or two additional PEEL paragraphs covering other relevant causes, consequences, or factors. Use comparative language to weigh these against the quoted factor.
> *"While X was important, Y was arguably more significant because..."*

### Conclusion — Decisive Judgement
The conclusion is where top marks are won or lost. A weak conclusion hedges ("all factors are equally important") — a strong conclusion:
1. Restates your overall judgement directly
2. Explains *why* the factor you chose ranks highest using your own criteria
3. Acknowledges the strongest counter-argument briefly
4. Ends with a precise historical insight, not a vague summary

> *"In conclusion, X was the most important cause because [specific reason]. While Y created the conditions for [outcome], it was X that directly precipitated [event]. Without X, Y alone would not have been sufficient to produce the same outcome — as evidenced by [specific comparison]."*

### SPaG (4 marks available on 16-mark essays)
To earn all 4 SPaG marks:
- Use precise historical vocabulary (e.g. "hyperinflation", "Enabling Act", "Lebensraum")
- Spell key names and terms correctly (e.g. Weimar, Hindenburg, Mussolini)
- Use clear paragraphing — no one-sentence paragraphs
- Use complex sentences with subordinate clauses, not a list of simple statements
- Proof-read the last 5 minutes of the exam

## Narrative Account Questions (8 marks)

These are *not* description questions — they require an *analytical narrative*.

**Wrong approach:** "First, X happened. Then Y happened. Then Z happened."

**Right approach:** Build a chain of causation — each event leads to the next.
> *"The Wall Street Crash of 1929 destroyed American confidence in laissez-faire economics, which led to widespread unemployment reaching 25% by 1933. This hardship drove voters to abandon the Republican Party, which enabled Franklin Roosevelt to win the 1932 election with his promise of a New Deal. His victory in turn gave him a powerful mandate to extend federal government intervention on an unprecedented scale."*

Use connective phrases: "This led to...", "As a consequence...", "This in turn...", "Which meant that...", "Triggering..."
